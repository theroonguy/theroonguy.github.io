## so it begins

about two week ago, i competed in my first 24-hour hackathon. itching to flex the game development muscles i've spent hundreds of hours building whilst working on tracerouter, i decided to commit to working no less than the entirety of the day and night to perfecting a winning game. a plan that surely works, no doubt.

after a boisterous introduction to the game jam by a robed president of UMBD's gamedev club, we received the theme: *royalty*. in my opinion, it was a difficult theme. it didn't immediately give unique, achievable concepts like other themes i've encountered (like "contamination" or "symmetry").

after some brainstorming, my team settled on a visual-novel, dialogue-driven card game about infiltrating an ancient chinese royal kingdom through careful manipulation and disguising. every communication with a person was a small card game---and every time you win someone's trust, you gain them as a card. and then, in future dialogues when a consort or a eunuch inevitably doesn't trust you, you can gain credibility by playing prior relationships with sufficient standing. and finally, you can make your way to the top of the chain and ultimately assassinate the emperor.

## how it ends.

in hindsight, this game concept never would have worked out. it wasn't just ambitious---it was an amorphous idea that didn't have clear direction. i've gotten very used to this trap throughout my time as a game developer, but the combination of a time crunch and a team that felt very excited about this concept made it hard to pay attention to the warning signs. i remember seeing the clear signs of this problem during our intial discussions. we would talk about how cards could be used to assassinate characters, or how you could disguise yourself with an item, or that there would be all these chains of stories where you could discover that the guard was secretly smuggling across the border and you could use that as leverage. unclear game mechanics such as "only having 3 interactions per day" only enabled more unstructured thinking. and it didn't help that i was planning on leaving the story to two people who were not used to game development. really, the story is deeply intertwined with the game design, and cannot be separated.

i ended up spending the entire night working, perhaps taking a brief nap for 15-20 minutes. i wasn't familiar with any dialogue systems and our concept had some (to my knowledge) unique characteristics, so i spend most of the time designing a custom dialogue system. it was a little brutal, but it also taught me a lot. i've made dialogue systems before, but i had never made one that was *entirely* custom-resource-based. i think it has a lot of potential for smaller projects, but i can see how it would become difficult for longer chains of dialogue. despite that, i would love to flesh out this system more properly for a much more intuitive and interactive dialogue tree.

i think a big challenge with the submission was leading a team of inexperienced devs. this may sound disparaging to my teammates, but i honestly view this as a problem *i* could've tackled better. it's not like they have any control over their prior experience. to be frank, i'm used to leading projects, and especially game development projects. in this case, it's because i have by far the most experience. but it's made me realize that there are so many ways to handle people with different skills, confidence levels, commitment, and ideas. there's a very delicate balance between striving to complete a quality product and letting people enjoy their time and learn what they want, and as the unofficial team leader, that's a balance that i have to weigh.

## never forget the whetstone

i feel like i can go on and on about the necessity of planning. as one of my favorite quotes that abe lincoln didn't say goes:

> "Give me 6 hours to chop down a tree and I will spend the first 4 sharpening the axe."

if there's anything i could change about the way i handled the jam, it would've been to plan more. admittedly, it was difficult because the rest of the team were keen to get moving, but really the lack of planning made coding slower, the story (which i needn't remind you is the core of the game) unprioritized, and the artwork undirected and underutilized. this is precisely the problem i've painfully sludged through when i spent a summer desperately trying to complete a game; when i come up with a game concept and keep the structure and boundaries unclear, it's like trying to contain milk that's already spilled on the ground. what *really* are the rules of a game mechanic? getting cards when gaining someone's trust and playing them in other conversations is a *premise*, not a *mechanic*. i don't have to know exactly what the final form of it is, but i have to have something that at least works and can be modified later on.

i think that it can be misleading to say that a good game sells an *experience* rather than content or a unique game mechanic. while it's very much true, to have an experience the content and game mechanics have to be fully thought out and structurally sound.
