Okay, so we already have neural networks and we're getting pretty good at making them. They're essentially massive quilts of nodes passing signals to each other and modifying themselves when it's suitable to do so. Like airplanes modeled after birds, camera lenses after our irises and wind turbines after whale flippers, we've modeled our digital brains after our human ones. 

Because ultimately, our brains are just a bunch of neurons that pass signals to each other, and which modify themselves when it's suitable to do so.

So: if we had limitless funding, energy and researchers to combine an unfathomable 86 billion digital neurons into one machine, could we just... make a brain? 

In fact, is that all our brains are? We have a few different types of neurons, broadly categorized under *sensory*, *motor*, and *interneurons*. But they really just function the same: They accept a range of inputs from neighboring neurons though dendrites, process them in a nucleus, and send outputs via an axon. Instead of digital signals, neurons play telephone using chemical neurotransmitters. Regardless of the medium, all neurons are are inputs, a weight, and an output. [NIH](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-life-and-death-neuron)

In fact, the parallels between our understanding of digital systems and our own brains even bleed into the language neuroscientists use to describe how our brain works:

It's a rather bleak image of what it means to be human; a body controlled by chain reactions that break down the soul into bits and bytes, each cell acting on repetition and reaction based on its immediate surroundings, unaware of any meaning or purpose. *It*, I said. Are we just made of many, tiny, *it*s?

Well, if there's anything I learned from my AP Psychology class in high school, it's that we know *barely anything* about how our bodies work.

Looking into it, it turns out that neurons might be the most diverse kinds of cells in the body, with hundreds of subtypes that send and receieve in different ways [NIH](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-life-and-death-neuron). We don't know if neurons communicate in chains, micro-circuits, or organized columns. A single neuron axon can have 1,000 branches that spread across the brain. Much of the processing in a neuron happens in the dendrites (the inputs), yet it still is rarely modeled at all in digital simulations. We have no idea how *intrinsic* activity in brains emerge---somehow, messy cellular noise across our brains across billions of neurons perfectly synchronize into massive, slow-moving waves that form dreams and deep thought and consume the vast majority of the brain's energy. [Roland](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10585277/)

We don't even know if our brains function using time as the independent variable. Of course, it's the most convenient option to record brain activity spikes according to a stopwatch, which is what the majority of neuroscience bases its research on. But there's experimental inconsistencies that suggest our neurons function based on spatial or state parameters. Time could very much be a subjective experience. In high-adrenaline situations, people report feeling like time slows down---it's entirely possible that for them, time really *does* slow down. [Roland](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10585277/)
